A working hypothesis for the Martian dichotomy

Charlotte Crocicchia's Mars research project · Version 1 · 24 September 2026

Research question: can one history of crustal growth, cooling and magnetic recording explain the structural and magnetic dichotomies while remaining compatible with the present interior?

This is a proposed research argument, not a fitted model or a discovery. It selects a tractable question from the evidence synthesis. The website is an interface to this argument; the observations and explicit assumptions must determine the outcome.

The idea to pursue

Working hypothesis: an early contrast in crustal structure helped create two different thermal and magnetic archives. Part of the magnetic dichotomy could therefore arise from when, where and how rocks recorded a shared planetary dynamo, without requiring a hemispherically stronger ancient field.

“Shared” means a common dynamo history with an explicit field geometry; even a dipole does not produce the same field strength at every location. This baseline does not assume that all southern rocks preserved their first magnetization. Both inheritance and later acquisition must be tested. It is our first model to challenge because it introduces fewer unconstrained field asymmetries, not because the evidence has already selected it.

The initial structural contrast could have been seeded by an impact, internal dynamics or a combination. Its subsequent evolution can partly obscure the trigger. We should first determine which recording histories are physically possible, then ask which origin models can produce them.

The causal proposal is early structural contrast → different thermal/compositional evolution → different acquisition and survival of magnetization → today's orbital signal. Dynamo history, source geometry and observational resolution enter independently. Later impacts, alteration, burial and erosion can modify several links.

A thick crust can contain more magnetic material, but it can also retain more heat. More crust therefore does not automatically mean more preserved magnetization. Heating can erase a record; cooling while a field is active can create a new one. The discriminant is the sequence of events at the source depth, not whether the south is simply “hotter.”

Two independent choices, four models to compare

These are endmembers for specified regions and source volumes. A heterogeneous planet can contain several histories; allowing an arbitrary mixture in every pixel would make the proposal untestable.

Model Dominant recording history Ancient field Distinguishing requirement
A — Inherited archive Early crust records a field, then retains much of that remanence Shared dynamo history Deep sources survive subsequent thermal and impact histories
B — Rebuilt archive Much of the original record is reset or new material is emplaced; a later record dominates Shared dynamo history Cooling or mineral formation overlaps a field-active interval and leaves sufficient coherent magnetization
C — Inheritance plus field asymmetry As in A Persistently stronger field in the south during relevant acquisition intervals A field contrast still improves independent predictions after recording and preservation are constrained
D — Rebuilding plus field asymmetry As in B Hemispheric field contrast during reacquisition Both a viable later recording window and the additional field geometry are required

A and B are our initial tests. C and D are explicit alternatives, not explanations invoked automatically whenever the simpler model misses a point. A hemispheric dynamo must also produce sufficient time-integrated coherence: some published simulations reverse too quickly for strong bulk remanence (Dietrich & Wicht, 2013). No probabilities or ranking of these four models have been calculated.

How this bears on origin scenarios

Northern excavation: inherited deep southern sources and loss of northern sources are compatible with this family. But the heating and burial of southern material by ejecta must be modeled too. Magnetic survival constraints have already been applied to this problem (Citron & Zhong, 2012). Their assumptions about ejecta and thermal evolution must be revisited before treating their result as an exclusion of every northern impact. A field that persists after cooling permits reacquisition.

Southern impact and crust production: a melt-producing event offers a route to thick crust, but a magnetic explanation needs cooling and carrier formation during an available field interval. The impact itself is not an explanation for preserved remanence (Ballantyne et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2024).

Internal growth and amplification: crustal growth and thermal feedback can link thickness and later thermal structure. These histories must also preserve old materials or explain their survival as local exceptions; deep intrusions and crustal differentiation can create or reset magnetic carriers. A modern hot mantle is not proof that a plume initiated the contrast (Bonnet Gibet et al., 2025).

Our useful constraint would be a set of permitted growth–heating–cooling–recording sequences. Each origin model must fit that same set, rather than fitting a different subset of observations.

The first study: when could the deep southern crust record a field?

Start with Terra Cimmeria and Terra Sirenum, where strong orbital anomalies motivate a source-depth test. Include northern lowland control regions with usable signal; do not interpret a failed depth inversion in a weak-field region as a shallow source.

  1. Reproduce regional magnetic spectra at a common altitude and bandwidth. Carry source-depth uncertainty and alternative source geometries; compare more than one field model where feasible.
  2. Use an ensemble of crustal thickness/density models and published thermal histories, with explicit carrier hypotheses. Require the same thermal parameters to explain crustal structure and magnetic survival/acquisition. Present mantle constraints are endpoint checks, not imposed ancient geotherms.
  3. Determine whether each source-volume history supports early inheritance, later recording, both, or neither. Propagate uncertainty in field-active intervals; do not fix a universally accepted dynamo shutdown date.
  4. Forward-predict magnetic observables with cooling-time and polarity cancellation, and test withheld regions. A thermally possible source is not automatically strong enough to explain the observed field.

First deliverable: a depth–time compatibility diagram, with an accompanying table of accepted and rejected histories and the observation responsible. No fitted diagram exists yet. The test protocol defines the inputs, decision rules and failure modes before analysis.

If inheritance fails but a later recording window works, we gain a conditional constraint on remagnetization. If both fail for reasonable carriers and thermal histories, that parameterized crustal history is inadequate. A stronger dynamo cannot rescue a volume that never records or retains remanence. If several histories remain viable, the honest result is a quantified degeneracy and a specification of the additional measurement needed.

Where meteorites, water and the atmosphere enter

Meteorites constrain mineral carriers, local event sequences and recording processes. NWA 7034 chemistry can remain useful even where hand magnets compromised its natural remanence; the nine tested paired stones are not nine independent Martian regions (Gattacceca et al., 2014; Vervelidou et al., 2023). ALH 84001 helps constrain possible field-active intervals, with its own age and recording interpretation. Neither meteorite family supplies a known global field geometry.

Alteration and water can change magnetic carriers; erosion, burial and volcanism can separate the visible boundary from its initial position. These are explicit recording/modification branches, not direct evidence for an ocean-caused crustal dichotomy. Surface mineral abundance is not assumed to represent deep sources (AlHantoobi et al., 2021; McNeil et al., 2025).

The wind laboratory has a narrower supporting role: surface-pressure harmonics can assess atmospheric loading corrections in time-variable gravity. Present winds do not explain the origin of an ancient crustal contrast. That calculation is a separate sensitivity test and has not yet been implemented; the research programme specifies the required time and surface conventions.

What is new to this project, and what is already published?

Thermal control of magnetization, impact survival tests, hemispheric dynamos and crustal feedback are established research directions. Ruiz, 2009 already relates early geotherms to candidate magnetic carriers. The 2026 tidal study itself discusses both thermal recording and dynamo asymmetry. We must not present their synthesis as our discovery.

Our proposed contribution is a reproducible consistency test using a common set of histories, explicitly joining source depth, carrier uncertainty, recording chronology and interior constraints. Its scientific novelty remains to be assessed against full texts and existing joint models. The present release provides the argument, competing assumptions and analysis specification; it has not ingested the required geological/magnetic datasets, fitted histories or selected an origin mechanism.
