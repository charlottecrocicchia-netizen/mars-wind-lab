A PERSONAL RESEARCH PROJECT · MARS · UPDATED 28 SEPTEMBER 2026

Mars has a magnetic south and a quiet north. We test why.

The southern highlands carry strong magnetic anomalies. The northern lowlands have weaker magnetic anomalies on average. Two explanations compete: the ancient magnetic field itself was stronger over the south, or the same field was recorded and preserved differently on each side. This site runs concrete, reproducible tests on public data and states what each one says.

Three results from the current data and models

3.8×

The southern mean field is 3.8 times the northern mean at 150 km altitude. Changing how the two sides are defined moves this ratio between 3.1 and 4.5; it never goes away.

7–41 km

The southern crust is thicker by this range across the declared density scenarios. This is our own gravity inversion, checked against the published archive to within 4.3 km.

×5 / ×6

At the two sites where the field has been measured on the ground, the orbital model continued to the surface is about 5 times too high at Zhurong and 6 times too low at InSight. Orbital models do not reliably resolve a local point field at the surface.

The idea under test

Our working hypothesis is that the magnetic dichotomy is mostly an archive effect: the north lost or never kept its record because its surface is younger, was reworked by impacts and volcanism, or cooled at the wrong time, while the south kept an old record. The alternative is that the dynamo itself produced a stronger field over the southern hemisphere. The tests below ask what the rocks, the heat and the measurements allow.

FOLLOW-UP RESULTS · SURFACE SUPPORT AND CRUSTAL MATERIALS

What the latest comparisons can resolve

The surface-based designs have reached a limit with the present inputs. Arabia matching lacks balanced coverage; the boundary comparison lacks enough supported blocks; source depth has no stable association with surface age under the declared sensitivities. We pause repeating these designs. This does not establish that surface geology is uninformative, that resurfacing is absent, or that excavation is preferred.

Six tests, six answers

Each test was declared in this repository before it was run, uses only public data, and can be reproduced with one command. Verdicts are about the tested scenario, not about the origin of the dichotomy as a whole. An internal implementation audit of the first run found two implementation errors and three over-statements; the numbers below come from the corrected build.

Test 1

Does surface age predict magnetic strength beyond crustal structure and the hemisphere itself?

Partly. Age has positive out-of-region skill in both wedge partitions (+0.13, +0.14), destroyed when the map is shifted in longitude. But a plain north/south indicator does as well (+0.17, +0.16) and adding age to it gains little. At equal epoch the north stays weaker: Middle Noachian terrain averages 20 nT north of the boundary and 49 nT south of it; the 23 Early Noachian northern cells have a similar aggregate (53 against 55 nT after back-transforming mean log amplitude), but 14 lie in Xanthe–Chryse alone.

Not decisive Surface age predicts part of the variation. A hemispheric association remains, but this does not identify the contribution of resurfacing.

Test 2

Could the deep southern crust have recorded a field before the dynamo stopped?

Early acquisition becomes less frequent with depth. In 66 accepted conductive histories, crust at 30 km last cooled through the magnetite Curie point at a median 4.09 Ga: before 4.1 Ga in 39% of histories, before 3.7 Ga in 59%, while 21% never reached that temperature and are uninformative. At 40 km the median is 3.50 Ga and only 30% cooled before 3.7 Ga. No accepted southern history gives a pyrrhotite crossing at 30 km before 3.7 Ga.

Adds a constraint Early crossings become less frequent with depth in this ensemble; neither a universal 30 km boundary nor a mineral exclusion follows.

Test 3

How strongly magnetized would the sources have to be?

In a declared cylinder scenario, a 20 km layer matching each window needs a median 4.2 A/m in the south (1.0 A/m in the north); 43% of southern windows exceed 5 A/m before any cancellation. If the record were acquired during slow cooling under randomly timed reversals every 0.67 Myr, the median would become 208 A/m.

Adds a constraint Several amperes per metre over tens of kilometres are needed in this geometry; cancellation during slow cooling would multiply that by tens to hundreds.

Test 4

Can slow cooling under a reversing dynamo keep a coherent record?

Only a small fraction. Cooling through the magnetite blocking band at 30 km took a median 774 Myr in the accepted southern histories. With randomly timed reversals at a tested rate motivated by conditional basin simulations (about 1.5 per Myr) the median retained fraction is 2.1% (95th percentile 6.3%); with perfectly periodic reversals it is 0.03%. The shortest tested mean chron giving at least 50% median retention is 500 Myr.

Adds a constraint The tested histories show strong median cancellation. Retention is a distribution, and this result does not exclude slow cooling generally.

Test 5

Does the orbital model predict the field measured on the ground?

Not at the two sites where it can be checked. At Zhurong the degree-134 model continued to the surface gives 79 nT; the rover measured 5.2–39.8 nT. At InSight the model gives 312 nT and the lander measured about 2,000 nT. The two errors go in opposite directions.

Adds a constraint Surface extrapolation of the orbital model failed by a factor of five to six at these comparisons (horizontal field at Zhurong, total field at InSight). That is a demonstrated local limit, not an uncertainty for every anomaly.

Test 6

Does a lighter southern crust remove the thickness dichotomy?

It shrinks it a lot. With equal densities the south is 24.1 km thicker than the north; with the Goossens & Sabaka densities the corrected inversion gives 8.4 km. The thinnest crust stays positive in every scenario (0.9 km).

Adds a constraint The contrast stays positive across the tested scenarios; its size depends strongly on density and the shared model assumptions.

What this means so far

  1. Surface age predicts part of the map, but leaves a hemispheric association. On both sides of the boundary the field falls with surface epoch: the global Early Noachian mean is 55 nT against 10 nT for Late Hesperian terrain. Yet at equal epoch the north remains two to four times weaker, except for a small Early Noachian sample. Fourteen of its 23 cells lie in Xanthe–Chryse; the regional decomposition and boundary transects document the limited support. These field summaries back-transform the area-weighted mean logarithm. This predictive comparison does not determine how much resurfacing caused the contrast.
  2. The tested slow-cooling histories show strong median cancellation. Deep crust cools through its blocking range over hundreds of millions of years. With randomly timed reversals every 0.67 Myr, the retained fraction at 30 km is about 2%; perfectly periodic reversals would leave far less. Under these assumptions, retaining strong sources requires more initial remanence or a different recording history. Faster cooling is one candidate; the calculation does not make it unique.
  3. Early cooling at depth depends on the assumed history. At 30 km, 39% of the accepted southern histories cross the magnetite Curie point before 4.1 Ga and 21% start below it, leaving earlier acquisition unresolved. Some deeper histories also cross early. These ensemble frequencies establish neither a hard depth boundary nor a probability for Mars.
  4. The magnetization budget is demanding in the tested geometry. Several amperes per metre over 20 km in a declared cylinder scenario; the numbers depend on that geometry and on the cap sizes of the source model, so they are a comparison scale, not a rock-type verdict.
  5. The crustal dichotomy is robust in sign; its size is not. Correcting the inversion for a laterally variable crust density changes the thickness contrast from 24 km to 8 km. Every origin model calibrated to a fixed contrast inherits this uncertainty.

What would reduce the ambiguity

  • Local magnetic surveys on Early Noachian terrain north of the boundary, with matched southern sites and geological context. A single point measurement would not establish a hemispheric source contrast.
  • Oriented, dated samples from a strong southern anomaly, to measure the carrier mineral and the cooling rate directly.
  • Southern seismology combined with composition and gravity, to reduce the density–thickness ambiguity.
  • A reversal chronology for the Martian dynamo between 4.5 and 3.7 Ga; recording predictions depend on reversal statistics, acquisition history and the duration of dynamo activity.

SYNTHESIS · 28 SEPTEMBER 2026

Where the evidence stands

The synthesis now includes six follow-ups and an internal implementation review. Rapid acquisition can preserve individual bodies; correlations in the shared field control how a stack combines. A synthetic counterexample shows that a changing reversal rate and different acquisition times can produce identical orbital fields. These results constrain combinations of histories and materials without selecting the origin of the dichotomy or dating a dynamo transition.
